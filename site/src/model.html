The research model

Machines decide what counts

Our results come out of a loop with five stages. A simulation suggests a law, a fitting engine names it, its predictions are locked in a ledger before the runs that test them, what survives is proved in Lean, and AI agents increasingly propose the mechanisms and write the proofs. At every stage a claim's status comes from a check that whoever made it, person or model, cannot change.

Why it is built this way

Two failures shape the design. The first is the old one of empirical science: with enough rules, sizes and candidate laws, some pattern will fit by chance, and choosing the test after seeing the data makes any law look good. The cure is to register. Before a single test size is computed, the predictions are written down, committed and pushed, and the harness refuses to start the run until they are.

The second failure is new. A language model can write a convincing account of work it did not do, or a proof that only looks right. So an agent's report is never the evidence. The evidence is a build that Lean accepts, a statement that matches a pinned copy, and an axiom list that is Lean's own three, checked by a judge the agent cannot edit.

Between them, these checks let us hand more of the work to agents without trusting them more.

Check a ledger yourself

Every registered study keeps an append-only ledger. Each entry holds the SHA-256 hash of the entry before it, and its own hash covers its content and that link, so changing any entry breaks every link after it. Below is the real ledger of the third pilot's registered test: the question, 20 predictions, the start of the run, the observations and the verdict. Your browser recomputes every hash now.

Edit an entry and the chain breaks there. A forger could recompute every later hash, and the Forge button does just that. This is why the ledger is pushed to GitHub before the run: the remote's history fixes every hash, so a rebuilt chain no longer matches what was published.

The agents, step by step

In each pilot a fresh agent gets a problem card, the Lean library and a few generic tools, in a cloud container that holds nothing else: an orphan branch with one commit, no write-ups and no hand proofs. It works alone. When it reports, the judge runs; if the judge rejects the work, only the judge's output goes back to the agent.

The chart shows every tool call the agents in pilots 2 to 6 made, read from the session transcripts on the server, not from anything the agents wrote. In pilots 2 and 3 the agents wrote their whole proof within ten minutes, then waited half an hour or more for mathlib to compile in their fresh containers. In pilots 4 and 5, on open questions, the agents had their mechanisms after 23 minutes and spent the rest of their time checking and proving them; the fifth also spent it looking for more, and reported what it could not explain. The sixth found its factorisation in 11 minutes and finished in 66. Pilot 1's agent ran inside the orchestrator's container, so it has no separate transcript.

Limits

What the pilots do not show

They show that the loop's mechanics work end to end, with real isolation, and that an agent can carry a technique to a new setting, find an unstated law from data, prove a case nobody had proved, and answer a question nobody had answered. They do not show that the hard questions are within reach. The fourth pilot's mechanism is elementary once seen, and the atlas's own tools could have found its key factor had anyone looked for factors shared between rules. Each library held close worked examples, and the agents said they followed them. The fifth pilot answered only part of its question, and left twelve cases unexplained. The sixth pilot's answer was a factorisation we had listed as unchecked and not tried. Six runs on six problems say little about how often the approach fails, or where it stops working.
