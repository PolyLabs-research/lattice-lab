Programme · Reckoner

Teaching a small model algebra by search, with a checker as the only judge

Reckoner asks whether a model of about five million parameters can learn multi-step algebra the way AlphaZero learned chess: by searching over moves and learning from what a verifier accepts, without large-scale pretraining. Every move is a rewrite from a fixed set of seven rules, so the model's reasoning is a derivation a person can read, and an unsound step cannot be taken at all.

The par game

A problem is an expression and a goal: work it out, simplify it, or solve it for x. A move applies one of seven rewrite rules (add, subtract or multiply two numbers, combine like terms, or add to, subtract from or divide both sides) at one place in the expression. Each rule is an equivalence, tested on 10,000 random instances, so no move can make the expression wrong. Numbers are written in base 625, the series' own numeral system, as two-digit tokens.

A checker decides when a problem is solved: exact evaluation for arithmetic, substitution for an equation, and agreement at random points for a simplification. Solving is not winning. Every problem carries a par, the length of a reference solution in the same seven rules, and the outcome is +1 for finishing in fewer steps, 0 for matching it, and −1 for taking more or hitting the step cap. Up to depth 6 par comes from an exhaustive search and cannot be beaten; deeper, it comes from a scripted solver and can be.

How it is judged

Each campaign and sweep is written down and approved before it runs, with its predictions and its rule for choosing. After that it changes only by dated amendments added at the end, and an amendment written after seeing a result has to say so. Every gate states four numbers: the floor, what a model that knows nothing scores, the threshold, and the measurement. That rule came from a gate that passed vacuously, below.

Every defect is written up, the small ones included, in a numbered list of findings (57 so far), and a finding that turns out wrong is withdrawn in place, not deleted.

The first campaign: under par more often

registered primary · 600 problems

Problems solved in fewer steps than par, out of the 600 deeper problems the campaign was registered on, at the start (the supervised anchor) and after iterations 4, 9, 14 and 19. Twenty iterations ran in 9.6 hours, with no alarms, and both no-regress floors held. The registered test compares the anchor with the final model: 101 against 209, a mean difference of 0.18, 95% interval 0.137 to 0.222.

What the gain is. Taken apart, it is shorter paths to solutions the anchor already found: 149 problems got shorter and 50 longer. On the tightest stratum, where beating par needs a genuinely shorter proof, the campaign went from 43 to 35, with an interval that includes zero. Six problems were newly solved, and all six landed exactly on par, which scores nothing. The measure cannot see a new solve at par, so the campaign's effect on capability is unmeasured.

What it trained on. Found eleven weeks later: the replay buffer stored the search's policy targets in the wrong coordinates, and 89–96% of their mass was dropped. The campaign improved without the target the loop exists to learn from. In the project's words, its gain "is real. Its cause is open."

A registered sweep where three of four predictions failed

13 November 2026

With the defects fixed, a sweep asked how much of each training batch must be old supervised data (the rehearsal fraction f) for the model not to forget what it knew. It predicted that with none, every seed would forget. Instead every arm held, and the arms with little or no rehearsal (f ≤ 0.15) gained the most, reading 1,208 to 1,212 against the anchor's 1,192.

Where it stands

The first campaign is closed and written up with its errata. The next is not yet registered, and no seed but one has been. Three one-change rounds are registered and not run, among them whether a rule the optimal derivations never use (it appears in 0 of 920) should stay in the set. The question the base-625 substrate was chosen to ask, whether numbers drawn as glyphs beat learned embeddings, has not been run either.

Reckoner is the second experiment in a series of small models. The first was the chess engine on the Tiny AlphaZero page, whose search it inherits. Later stages are planned and not specified: a logic specialist checked by a proof checker, a router that only dispatches, so that what the system says stays checkable, and last, a model of language with no verifier at all.
