Programme · Protocell

One cell, evolved to manage light: does behaviour come from parts working together?

Protocell builds the simplest organism it can, a single simulated cell whose wall and brain are one small neural network, and asks whether its behaviour emerges from the interaction of its parts. It starts with few parts, each understood, and adds complexity only when a test needs it. Light is the cell's food, and it has to find a good place in the light and hold it.

The hypothesis, in two senses

In the weak sense, the cell's behaviour depends on its parts working together: remove any internal part and the behaviour breaks. In the strong sense, a cell of interacting parts does better than every simple mechanism. The simple mechanisms are written as rules and run in the same world as baselines. The reflex beats harder on its brighter side. Run and wait swims straight while the light it feels is rising and waits otherwise. Read and run aims each run by reading the light across its wall. The strongest, called B8, uses run-and-wait's trigger with read-and-run's aim.

A cell's fitness is its mean energy reserve over a lifetime of a day or two, with the ticks after death counting zero, on a scale from 0 to 1. Every world must pass its own checks before any cell evolves in it: sitting still must fail, the light must be sensable and reachable, random wiring must rarely work, and runs must repeat bit for bit. A preregistration with predictions comes before every build and run, and results are corrected by dated notes, never rewritten.

The road to beating the best simple rule

For three stages a simple rule sat within 2–3% of the best any cell could do, so the strong sense could not be tested. Stage 4a built a world with room: a tighter economy, where B8 scores 0.576 and the best a still cell can do at the brightest point is 0.694. Then, one approach at a time:

The numbers in this chart

Each row is a separate test on its own fresh starts, with B0 and B8 run on the same starts, which is why their scores move a little between rows. On the cell's first way of sensing, evolution from nothing got no further than 0.241, far below B8; fitting the cell to B8's beats and cells written weight by weight stayed at the still cell's level. Changing what the cell senses, to light relative to its recent level, let a cell written weight by weight carry 0.909 of B8's lead over the still cell, and a search starting there, moving each gene about 1%, found cells that beat B8. Then evolution from random genomes on the new cell, otherwise the search that had failed: at 200 generations two of three runs did B8's job, one beat it and one ended as the still cell; at 400, four of five did its job and two beat it.

The strong sense, in one world, with its caveats. In stage 4a's world, two of three cells searched from a designed start beat B8 on 100 fresh starts: 0.610 and 0.593 against 0.557. Evolution from random genomes on the same cell then did B8's job in six of eight preregistered runs and beat it in three: one of three at 200 generations and two of five at 400, where the criteria asked for two and three. The best, 0.642 against B8's 0.558, is the best cell so far. Twice a preregistration predicted that no champion would beat B8, and twice one did; stage 6b's predicted that three of five would, and two did.

The caveats: by its own criterion the strong sense is not shown from nothing. It is one world, one spoke mode and one way of sensing; the sensor, and the path that eases the world in, were designed by hand; and B8 is only the best simple rule tried. Away from home the lead mostly goes: in six worlds they never saw, the four champions beat each world's best simple rule, re-tuned there, in 4 of 24 pairs.

Probes read how the cells do it. The search from the design retuned it rather than rebuilt it: the core's links carry about two thirds of the gain, and its champion starts 86% of its fresh runs while the light is rising, against 51% for the hand-written cell. Evolution from random genomes found another way. Its cells need the ring's links, which the design does not use, and the one read swims in circles almost all day, biasing the circling toward the light by means not yet shown.

Where it stands

Stages 4 to 7 are written up. In stage 4a's world, evolution from random genomes on the cell that senses relative light reliably finds a cell that does the best simple rule's job, and in a minority of runs one that beats it. Every run was still improving at 400 generations; whether more would carry the others past B8 is not known. In worlds the cells never saw, the simple rules, with one setting re-tuned for each world, hold up better than cells made in one world. One evolved cell closes at least 0.81 of the gap at home and in all six; why it does, and its sibling from the same search does not, has not been read.

The next stage, being drafted, evolves cells in several worlds at once, asking whether a generalist emerges that beats each world's best simple rule, tested also in worlds held back. Also open: more generations for stage 6b's runs, how the evolved cell's circling is biased, the cell with no front on the new sensing, and the plan's stage on damage from too much light, which has not been run.
