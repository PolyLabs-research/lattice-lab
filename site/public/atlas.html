The linear atlas

Build a rule from rings of neighbours, called shells, and add mod 2. Every such rule is linear, so its period on a torus is decided by algebra. We asked Kepler for laws of 42 of them, registered the pattern behind those laws, and refined it twice, each time because a registered prediction failed.

Why: eigenvalues in a finite field

On the torus (Z/m)² with m odd, a rule f acts diagonally. Its eigenvalues are f(ζj, ζk), where ζ is a primitive m-th root of unity in the field F(2d) and d = ordm(2). The period's odd part is the lcm of their multiplicative orders. That bridge is a theorem in our Lean library (period_eq_eigenLcm).

Each square of the map is one eigenvalue. Squares in the same Frobenius orbit, (j, k) → (2j, 2k), are conjugates and share an order. A symmetric rule's eigenvalues lie in F(2sord(m)), so its period divides the full bound 2sord(m) − 1. A rule falls short of it only if something keeps every eigenvalue inside a proper subgroup.

What holds a rule below the bound

Four mechanisms confine the eigenvalues, and each is a polynomial identity over GF(2) that you can multiply out below: splitting into one-dimensional passes, powers, dilations and twisted powers. The triangular lattice adds one more fact: each rule's 2 × 2 matrix squares to a plane rule s times the identity, so two triangle steps are one step of s.

Identities, multiplied out mod 2

Each square is a translation the rule reads, and the ring marks the origin. A product of rules is the sum of all pairwise translations, kept where it occurs an odd number of times.

Coincidences, and one that is not

In a small field a rule with few distinct eigenvalues can have all of them in a subgroup by chance. The census below takes all 1,274 shell rules of the atlas, on all three lattices, at every odd m where the cubes are a proper subgroup. It counts the rules whose every eigenvalue is a cube although no known mechanism accounts for it. The count falls away as the field grows, as chance would, except at m = 37.

The cubes at m = 37

At m = 37 the full bound is 218 − 1 = 262,143 = 3 × 87,381. Six rules that none of the four mechanisms touches have every eigenvalue a cube there, and period 87,381, which is rule 90's own period at 37:

  • square A4 + K8 + B4 + self
  • triangle VS + T9 + T13 + self
  • square K8 + M8 + E4
  • square C4 + L8 + M8
  • square C4 + L8 + M8 + E4
  • square A4 + B4 + C4 + L8 + E4 + self

Each has only 9 to 11 distinct eigenvalues up to Frobenius, so chance would do this about once in 20,000 to 180,000 rules. It happened six times among 1,274, and none of the six falls short at any other odd size from 9 to 161.

The fourth pilot set an agent on this question, in its own container, with the census above up to m = 161 and the Lean library. Nobody knew the answer. It found a mechanism, half-Frobenius confinement, that explains all six, and proved it in Lean. Its general condition made 633 claims at 94 sizes, 180 of them at sizes it never saw, and none was false.

Rule names are the atlas's neighbour shells: N4 the four nearest cells, D4 the diagonals, A4, B4, C4, K8, L8, M8 and E4 further square shells, H1 the hexagon's six neighbours, and E, VO, VS, T9 and T13 the triangle's.

Every number on this site comes from the project's registered records or is computed in your browser from the rules themselves. Experiments run on LatticeLife; laws are fitted by Kepler; both are open source. The studies, ledgers and Lean library are in lattice-lab, which is private for now.

The agents in the pilots are Claude Code sessions, each started fresh with no context but a problem card. Their proofs count because Lean accepts them, not because they say so.
