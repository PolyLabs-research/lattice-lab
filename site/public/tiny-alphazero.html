Programme · Tiny AlphaZero

How strong can a 6.5-million-parameter chess network get on one small computer?

Tiny AlphaZero rebuilds the AlphaZero loop (a network that plays itself, guided by tree search, and learns from the games) at the smallest size that is still complete, on one mini-PC. Its first version could not tell whether it was improving. This one is built around that question: a fixed ladder of frozen opponents, intervals on every score, and one change per round, registered before it runs.

Network
6,536,199 parameters: an 8-layer transformer reading the board as 72 tokens
Rounds
30, p2_a to p2_ae, August to November 2026: 23 of self-play, two benchmarks, five on Stockfish's labels alone
Strength now
0.5280 against Stockfish 16 at skill 5, its first score above 0.5, from 0.3810 before p2_aa; not yet placed on the CCRL scale

How improvement is measured

The first version decided which network was best by four-game matches, so its "best model" changed by coin flip. Here progress is read against a fixed ladder: frozen opponents, from earlier networks to Stockfish 16 held to a set skill level, playing 100 four-move openings, each twice with colours swapped. Every score comes with a 95% interval from a paired bootstrap over the openings, and a round's verdict compares two networks opening by opening.

Each round changes one thing. Its file is committed before launch, with the hypothesis, the primary test, forecasts and a table of what each outcome would decide. A gate is never moved to let a network through: when two early gates proved to measure the wrong thing, they were retired to reported-only with the argument written down, and their thresholds were left where they were.

The network starts from a supervised warm start, and is trained by a cycle of 512 self-play games, searched at 48 simulations a move, then 400 training steps. Every measurement runs on the mini-PC, which has an integrated AMD GPU; from late October the self-play training ran on rented GPUs. It is not pure self-play: mate puzzles are rehearsed from the second round, and from round p2_t a quarter of every batch is positions labelled by Stockfish. Since p2_aa the network has been trained on Stockfish's labels alone, with no self-play games: millions of positions its earlier self-play reached, each seen a few times, and from p2_ac positions from rated human games on Lichess as well. That training runs on the mini-PC; the labelling is rented, as were the games the standing network played against itself in p2_ae to supply new positions.

Where it stands on a public scale: a spread, not a number

1,400 games, registered

To place the network on CCRL's rating list, the network as it stood after p2_w played seven opponents of published rating, 200 games each. The registered test asked whether the classical engines agree among themselves about its rating. None of the three agrees with the other two, so the decision table's answer is that there is no single number at this level, and the page publishes each opponent's reading instead.

Performance is the rating the results imply against an opponent of known rating, with a 95% interval where the run log gives one; the four Stockfish settings together fit 1,674. The registered next step on this track is to rerun the opponents at a clock scaled to CCRL's reference machine before any one rating is quoted. These readings are of the network before p2_aa; the one trained since has not been placed.

Where it stands

The plateau after p2_t has ended, on one route. Trained on Stockfish-labelled positions alone, four million positions each seen about four times beat the start at skill 5 in p2_aa, a second run of a gain the capacity-or-data diagnostic had found in an aside; it was the same positions in a new order, so the data were not replicated. In p2_ab eight million positions beat that training by +0.0232, with the interval's lower end at +0.0011, on one run. In p2_ac 31.9 million positions beat the eight-million network by +0.0875 (+0.0694 to +0.1055), the largest gain between two rounds the project has measured. The added 23,892,957 were 8,845,607 from older self-play and 15,047,350 from rated human games on Lichess. The network that now stands scores 0.5280 against skill 5, the first above 0.5. Against the start the gain is +0.1470, reported rather than registered.

Size and source changed together in p2_ac, so that round did not say which paid. p2_ad held size equal, at 16,845,607 positions, and split the source. Under the project's training rule the older self-play was worth more, position for position: the network trained on the human games scored 0.0301 lower (−0.0467 to −0.0135), where a gain had been forecast, and the one trained on the self-play alone was not measurably weaker than p2_ac's network on all 31.9 million. In p2_ae the standing network played itself for 8,845,607 fresh positions. Added to p2_ad's self-play set they gave no measurable gain, −0.0127 (−0.0294 to +0.0041), and in place of the older self-play they trained a weaker network. p2_ac's network still stands.

Both rounds carry one caveat. Training stops when the network stops improving on held-out positions, all of them from older self-play, and three times a set unlike that one stopped far earlier: at 20.75, 20.5 and 34.0 million samples against 45.0 million. The comparisons are between networks trained for different lengths, and the rounds cannot say how much of the difference that carries. The next round, p2_af, is to change the training, not the data: the stopping rule, or a lower learning rate after it. It is not yet chosen or registered. Why many positions each seen a few times help, when a small set drilled many times hurt, is a hypothesis, and the diagnostic's own question, size or data, is not resolved. On the benchmark track the clock-scaled rerun is registered and not scheduled, and the network that now stands has not been placed on the CCRL scale. Reckoner carries the same search, and the same rules for registering a round, over to algebra.
