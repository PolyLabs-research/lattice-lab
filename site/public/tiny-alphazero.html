Programme · Tiny AlphaZero

How strong can a 6.5-million-parameter chess network get on one small computer?

Tiny AlphaZero rebuilds the AlphaZero loop (a network that plays itself, guided by tree search, and learns from the games) at the smallest size that is still complete, on one mini-PC. Its first version could not tell whether it was improving. This one is built around that question: a fixed ladder of frozen opponents, intervals on every score, and one change per round, registered before it runs.

How improvement is measured

The first version decided which network was best by four-game matches, so its "best model" changed by coin flip. Here progress is read against a fixed ladder: frozen opponents, from earlier networks to Stockfish 16 held to a set skill level, playing 100 four-move openings, each twice with colours swapped. Every score comes with a 95% interval from a paired bootstrap over the openings, and a round's verdict compares two networks opening by opening.

Each round changes one thing. Its file is committed before launch, with the hypothesis, the primary test, forecasts and a table of what each outcome would decide. A gate is never moved to let a network through: when two early gates proved to measure the wrong thing, they were retired to reported-only with the argument written down, and their thresholds were left where they were.

The network starts from a supervised warm start, and is trained by a cycle of 512 self-play games, searched at 48 simulations a move, then 400 training steps. Every measurement runs on the mini-PC, which has an integrated AMD GPU; since late October the training has run on rented GPUs. It is not pure self-play: mate puzzles are rehearsed from the second round, and since round p2_t a quarter of every batch is positions labelled by Stockfish.

The numbers in this chart

Each round's two arms, each scored over six 200-game passes of the openings against the same opponent on the same machine. Of seven changes, one helped (a Stockfish teacher in a quarter of each batch), one hurt (twice the teacher), and five made no difference the test could separate. The best six-pass score is 0.379, and no round since p2_t has separated from its own start.

Where it stands on a public scale: a spread, not a number

1,400 games, registered

To place the network on CCRL's rating list, one network played seven opponents of published rating, 200 games each. The registered test asked whether the classical engines agree among themselves about its rating. None of the three agrees with the other two, so the decision table's answer is that there is no single number at this level, and the page publishes each opponent's reading instead.

The numbers in this chart

Performance is the rating the results imply against an opponent of known rating, with a 95% interval where the run log gives one; the four Stockfish settings together fit 1,674. The network searched 48 simulations a move, about 0.12 s, while its opponents took about 1 to 1.9 s a move. The registered next step on this track is to rerun the opponents at a clock scaled to CCRL's reference machine before any one rating is quoted.

Where it stands

Strength against skill 5 has been flat since p2_t, and the levers tried on top of the teacher are closed. The next diagnostic, drafted and not yet run, asks whether the network's distance from its teacher is set by its size or by how few labelled positions it has seen. On the benchmark track, the clock-scaled rerun is registered as the next step and not scheduled. Reckoner carries the same search, and the same rules for registering a round, over to algebra.
