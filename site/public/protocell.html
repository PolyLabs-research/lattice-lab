Programme · Protocell

One cell, evolved to manage light: does behaviour come from parts working together?

Protocell builds the simplest organism it can, a single simulated cell whose wall and brain are one small neural network, and asks whether its behaviour emerges from the interaction of its parts. It starts with few parts, each understood, and adds complexity only when a test needs it. Light is the cell's food, and it has to find a good place in the light and hold it.

The hypothesis, in two senses

In the weak sense, the cell's behaviour depends on its parts working together: remove any internal part and the behaviour breaks. In the strong sense, a cell of interacting parts does better than every simple mechanism. The simple mechanisms are written as rules and run in the same world as baselines. The reflex beats harder on its brighter side. Run and wait swims straight while the light it feels is rising and waits otherwise. Read and run aims each run by reading the light across its wall. The strongest, called B8, uses run-and-wait's trigger with read-and-run's aim.

A cell's fitness is its mean energy reserve over a lifetime of a day or two, with the ticks after death counting zero, on a scale from 0 to 1. Every world must pass its own checks before any cell evolves in it: sitting still must fail, the light must be sensable and reachable, random wiring must rarely work, and runs must repeat bit for bit. A preregistration with predictions comes before every build and run, and results are corrected by dated notes, never rewritten.

The road to beating the best simple rule

stage 4a's world · fresh starts

For three stages a simple rule sat within 2–3% of the best any cell could do, so the strong sense could not be tested. Stage 4a built a world with room: a tighter economy, where B8 scores 0.576 and the best a still cell can do at the brightest point is 0.694. Then, one approach at a time:

The numbers in this chart

Each row is a separate test on its own fresh starts, with B0 and B8 run on the same starts, which is why their scores move a little between rows. Evolution from nothing got no further than 0.241, far below B8; fitting the cell to B8's beats and cells written weight by weight stayed at the still cell's level. Changing what the cell senses, to light relative to its recent level, let a cell written weight by weight carry 0.909 of B8's lead over the still cell. A search starting there, moving each gene about 1%, found cells that beat B8.

The strong sense, shown once, with its caveats. In stage 4a's world, two of three searched cells beat B8 on 100 fresh starts: 0.610 and 0.593 against 0.557, surviving 100% and 96% of starts. The preregistration had predicted that no champion would beat B8. But the start was designed, not evolved, so this does not show such a cell emerging from nothing; it is one spoke mode, one world and one way of sensing; the third champion does not clear the criterion; and B8 is only the best simple rule tried.

An explaining probe found the search had retuned the designed mechanism rather than rebuilt it: the core's links carry about two thirds of the gain, and the champion crosses the dark about twice as fast and holds a rising run more firmly.

Where it stands

Stages 4 and 5 are written up. The open question the result leaves is the one its caveats name: whether a cell that beats the simple rules can emerge from nothing on the new way of sensing, which is the next stage to be drafted. Also open: how the champions' fresh runs come to begin aimed at the light, the cell with no front on the new sensing, and the plan's stage on damage from too much light, which has not been run.

Every number on this site comes from a programme's own records (its registered predictions, preregistrations, run logs and rulings) or is computed in your browser. Where a test failed, the page says so, and a result counts because a check outside the work passed it, not because the work says so.
