## The reason

Write $v=f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)$. Two elementary facts drive everything.

**(i) Values lie in $F_{2^{18}}$.** Since $f$ has $\mathrm{GF}(2)$ coefficients, Frobenius gives $f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)^2=f(\zeta^{2j},\zeta^{2k})$. As $2$ has order $36$ mod $37$ and $2^{18}\equiv-1$, the $(x,y)\mapsto(1/x,1/y)$‑symmetry gives $v^{2^{18}}=f(\zeta^{-j},\zeta^{-k})=v$, so $v\in F_{2^{18}}$.

**(ii) "Cube" = "norm to $F_4$ is $1$".** In $F_{2^{18}}^\*$ (cyclic of order $2^{18}-1=3^3\cdot7\cdot19\cdot73$) the cubes are the index‑3 subgroup, and the cubic character is exactly the norm to $F_4$:
$$\chi(v)=v^{(2^{18}-1)/3}=v^{\,1+4+16+\cdots+4^8}=\prod_{i=0}^{8}v^{4^i}=N_{F_{2^{18}}/F_4}(v)\in F_4 .$$
Because $4^i=2^{2i}$ is a power of Frobenius, $v^{4^i}=f(\zeta^{4^i j},\zeta^{4^i k})$. Hence the cubic character of the value is itself a **Laurent polynomial in $f$**:
$$\boxed{\ \chi\!\big(f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)\big)=\Big(\textstyle\prod_{i=0}^{8} f(x^{4^i},y^{4^i})\Big)\Big|_{x=\zeta^j,\,y=\zeta^k}\ }$$
with multipliers $4^i\bmod37=\{1,4,16,27,34,25,26,30,9\}$.

So "every nonzero value is a cube" is literally the statement that this product is $1$ wherever $f\neq0$. As a polynomial congruence over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$ modulo $(x^{37}-1,y^{37}-1)$ this is:
$$f\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{8} f(x^{4^i},y^{4^i})\ \equiv\ f ,\qquad\text{(and the sharper }\textstyle\prod_{i=0}^8 f(x^{4^i},y^{4^i})\equiv1\text{ when $f$ has no zero values).}$$
Both are **verified**: the sharp form holds for $f_1,f_5$; the uniform form holds for all five.

## General condition (stated, with proof)

**Theorem.** Let $f$ be a Laurent polynomial over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$ invariant under $(x,y)\mapsto(1/x,1/y)$. Let $m$ be odd, $\zeta$ a primitive $m$-th root of unity, and suppose $2$ has order $2d\bmod m$ with $2^{d}\equiv-1\pmod m$ (so all values lie in $F_{2^d}$, by (i)). Let $e\mid d$, put $q=2^{e}-1$ and
$$P_{e}f(x,y):=\prod_{i=0}^{d/e-1} f\!\big(x^{2^{ei}},y^{2^{ei}}\big).$$
Then **every nonzero value $f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)$ is a $q$-th power in $F_{2^d}^\*$** $\iff$
$$f\cdot P_{e}f\ \equiv\ f\pmod{x^{m}-1,\;y^{m}-1},$$
and if additionally $f$ has no zero values this is equivalent to $P_{e}f\equiv1$.

*Proof.* Since $e\mid d$, $q=2^e-1\mid2^d-1$, the $q$-th powers are the index‑$q$ subgroup of $F_{2^d}^\*$, and $v\mapsto v^{(2^d-1)/q}=N_{F_{2^d}/F_{2^e}}(v)$ is a surjection $F_{2^d}^\*\to F_{2^e}^\*$ with kernel the $q$-th powers. Now $(2^d-1)/q=\sum_{i=0}^{d/e-1}2^{ei}$, and $v^{2^{ei}}=f(\zeta^{2^{ei}j},\zeta^{2^{ei}k})$, so $P_ef(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)=N_{F_{2^d}/F_{2^e}}(v)$. Thus $v$ is a $q$-th power $\iff v=0$ or $N(v)=1\iff v\,(P_ef(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)+1)=0$. The ring $\mathrm{GF}(2)[x,y]/(x^m-1,y^m-1)$ is reduced ($m$ odd ⇒ separable) and its points are exactly the $(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)$; a polynomial vanishing at all of them is $\equiv0$. Hence the pointwise statement for all $(j,k)$ is equivalent to $f\cdot(P_ef+1)\equiv0$, i.e. $f\cdot P_ef\equiv f$. $\square$

For the five polynomials: $m=37,\ d=18,\ e=2,\ q=3,\ d/e=9$ — exactly the identities above. This **proves** cubeness at $37$ via a finite algebraic identity (independent of, and corroborated by, the exhaustive point check).

**An equivalent but non-illuminating certificate.** Because *all* 38 nonzero orbits of $(j,k)\mapsto(2j,2k)$ have length $36$, a Frobenius‑consistent cube root always descends: there is an $\iota$‑symmetric $g\in\mathrm{GF}(2)[x^\pm,y^\pm]$ with $g\cdot g^{(2)}\equiv f$ (so $v=g(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)^3$ with $g(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)\in F_{2^{18}}$). I constructed such $g$ for all five (coefficients came out in $\{0,1\}$, confirming existence), but each has ~700 terms — a valid proof, not an explanation.
