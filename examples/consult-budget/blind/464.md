# The reason: cube ⇔ norm‑to‑𝔽₄ is 1, and that norm is a single GF(2) polynomial identity

## Setup facts (proved)

Write $R=\mathbb F_2[x,y]/(x^{37}-1,\,y^{37}-1)$, and let $\zeta$ be a primitive $37$‑th root of unity. Evaluation $\mathrm{ev}_{j,k}:R\to\mathbb F_{2^{36}}$, $x\mapsto\zeta^j,\ y\mapsto\zeta^k$, is a ring homomorphism, and $\mathrm{ev}_{j,k}(f)=f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)$.

**Lemma 1 (every value lands in $\mathbb F_{2^{18}}$).**
$\operatorname{ord}_{37}(2)=36$ and $2^{18}\equiv-1\pmod{37}$ (checked: `pow(2,18,37)=36`). The Frobenius $\Phi\colon\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{2^{18}}$ generates $\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb F_{2^{36}}/\mathbb F_{2^{18}})$. For any $f$ with $\mathbb F_2$‑coefficients that is invariant under $(x,y)\mapsto(x^{-1},y^{-1})$,
$$f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)^{2^{18}}=f(\zeta^{2^{18}j},\zeta^{2^{18}k})=f(\zeta^{-j},\zeta^{-k})=f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k),$$
so every value is $\Phi$‑fixed, i.e. in $\mathbb F_{2^{18}}$. (Same for $t(x)=x+x^{-1}$.) *Confirmed computationally for all five: `all_in_F2^18 = True`.*

**Lemma 2 (cube ⇔ norm‑one).**
$2\mid 18$, so $\mathbb F_4\subset\mathbb F_{2^{18}}$. The norm $N:=N_{\mathbb F_{2^{18}}/\mathbb F_4}$ is
$$N(\alpha)=\prod_{i=0}^{8}\alpha^{4^i}=\alpha^{\frac{4^9-1}{4-1}}=\alpha^{\frac{2^{18}-1}{3}}=\alpha^{87381}.$$
$N\colon\mathbb F_{2^{18}}^*\to\mathbb F_4^*$ is onto with kernel the **unique index‑3 subgroup = the cubes**. Hence
$$\boxed{\ \alpha\in\mathbb F_{2^{18}}^*\text{ is a cube}\iff N(\alpha)=1\iff\alpha^{87381}=1.\ }$$
(Here $87381=\tfrac{2^{18}-1}{3}=3^2\cdot 7\cdot 19\cdot 73$.) *Proved.*

**Lemma 3 (the norm is one ring element).**
Because $f\in\mathbb F_2[x^{\pm},y^{\pm}]$, Frobenius‑squared gives $f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)^4=f(\zeta^{4j},\zeta^{4k})$. With $\alpha=f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)\in\mathbb F_{2^{18}}$ (Lemma 1, so $\alpha^{4^9}=\alpha^{2^{18}}=\alpha$, and the nine factors are exactly the $\mathbb F_4$‑conjugates):
$$N\big(f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)\big)=\prod_{i=0}^{8}f(\zeta^{4^i j},\zeta^{4^i k})=P_f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k),\qquad
P_f:=\prod_{i=0}^{8}f\!\left(x^{4^i},y^{4^i}\right)\in R,$$
where $4^i\bmod37\in\{1,4,16,27,34,25,26,30,9\}$ (and $4^9\equiv-1$). *Proved; `P(zeta)==alpha^87381 : True` for all five.*

## The general condition (necessary **and** sufficient)

$R\cong\prod_c\mathbb F_{2^{d_c}}$ over the Frobenius ($\times2$) orbits $c$ of pairs $(j,k)$, and an element $r\in R$ satisfies $r^2=r$ **iff every component value lies in $\mathbb F_2=\{0,1\}$**. By Lemma 3 the component values of $P_f$ are exactly the numbers $N(f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k))\in\mathbb F_4\cup\{0\}$, which is $0$ iff the value is $0$. Therefore:

> **Theorem.** Let $f\in\mathbb F_2[x^{\pm},y^{\pm}]$ be invariant under $(x,y)\mapsto(x^{-1},y^{-1})$, and let $m=37$. Then
> $$\text{every nonzero }f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)\text{ is a cube in }\mathbb F_{2^{18}}^*
> \iff P_f^{\,2}\equiv P_f \pmod{x^{37}-1,\ y^{37}-1,\ 2}.$$
> The right side is a **finite GF(2) identity in $R$**. *Proved (equivalence).*

**General $(q-1)$‑th‑power form.** For any odd $m$, let $d=\operatorname{ord}_m(2)$, and suppose all values of $f$ lie in a subfield $\mathbb F_{2^e}$ (e.g. forced by a symmetry with $2^e\equiv\pm1\pmod m$). Fix $q=2^r$ with $r\mid e$ (so $q-1\mid 2^e-1$). Put $P_f^{(q)}=\prod_{i=0}^{e/r-1}f(x_1^{q^i},\dots,x_n^{q^i})$. Then
$$\text{every nonzero value is a }(q-1)\text{-th power in }\mathbb F_{2^e}^*\iff \big(P_f^{(q)}\big)^2\equiv P_f^{(q)}\ \text{ in }\mathbb F_2[\mathbf x]/(x_l^m-1).$$
This is exactly Lemma 2/Theorem with $N_{\mathbb F_{2^e}/\mathbb F_q}$ in place of $N_{\mathbb F_{2^{18}}/\mathbb F_4}$; the $(q-1)$‑th powers and the norm kernel coincide because $\mathbb F_{2^e}^*$ is cyclic. The cube case is $(m,e,q)=(37,18,4)$.

- **Separate the two mechanisms.** "All nonzero values are cubes" is *vacuous* at any $m$ where the ambient field degree $e$ is odd (then $3\nmid 2^e-1$). The registry should record $e$ and only count moduli with $3\mid 2^e-1$ as genuine hits; otherwise the "rare at other moduli" statement is partly comparing against trivially‑true cases.
- **Register the identity, not the sample.** The content is the *finite* GF(2) identity $P_f^2\equiv P_f$ (and $P_{f1}\equiv P_{f5}\equiv1$). - **Pin the "1 in 20000".** My random ensemble (unions of $2$–$6$ orbits in the fundamental domain) gave a hit rate near $1/136$, not $1/20000$ — the figure is extremely sensitive to the ensemble. The number $3^9\approx19683$ is suggestive (the norm is a product of $9$ conjugates), but the heuristic needs a precisely specified model to be reproducible. I'd flag the current figure as model‑dependent.
- **The deeper "why" is still open.** I proved the *equivalent* condition and verified it, but I have **no structural reason** why these particular $f_i$ make $P_f$ idempotent (and $P_{f1},P_{f5}\equiv1$). This is the right next target.
