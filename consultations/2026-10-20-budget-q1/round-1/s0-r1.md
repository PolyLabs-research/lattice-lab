## The reason: f is a cube in the group algebra

### The key reduction

Work in the ring
$$R_m \;=\; \mathrm{GF}(2)[x^{\pm1},y^{\pm1}]\big/(x^m-1,\;y^m-1).$$
Because $x^m-1$ is separable over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$, $R_m$ is **étale**: it is a finite product of finite fields, one factor for each Frobenius orbit of points $(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)$, and evaluation $f\mapsto f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)$ is projection onto that factor.

Therefore:

> **Lemma 1.** For $f\in R_m$, *every* value $f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)$ is a cube in its field (with $0=0^3$ counting as a cube) **iff $f$ is a cube in $R_m$.**

This is just "a tuple is a cube iff each coordinate is," applied to $R_m\cong\prod_i \mathbb F_{q_i}$. So the whole phenomenon is the single algebraic statement *"$f$ is a cube."* Nothing about $m=37$ is special except that it makes it happen.

### Making it an honest polynomial identity (Freshman's dream)

Over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$, squaring is the Frobenius substitution:
$$g(x,y)^2 \equiv g(x^2,y^2)\pmod{2}.$$
Hence $g^3=g\cdot g^2$ gives

> **Lemma 2.** $f$ is a cube in $R_m$ iff there is $g\in R_m$ with
> $$\boxed{\,f(x,y)\;\equiv\;g(x,y)\,\cdot\,g(x^2,y^2)\pmod{x^m-1,\;y^m-1}\,}$$

This is a *finite, machine-checkable* identity — no exponentiation by $(2^{18}-1)/3$ is needed.

### Getting the cube in the **right** field ($\mathbb F_{2^{18}}$, not $\mathbb F_{2^{36}}$)

Each $f_i$ and $t$ is invariant under $\sigma:(x,y)\mapsto(1/x,1/y)$ (every $O(a,b)$ is closed under sign flips), so $f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)\in\mathbb F_{2^{18}}$ (it is fixed by $\mathrm{Frob}^{18}=\sigma$, since $2^{18}\equiv-1\bmod 37$). To conclude the value is a cube *inside $\mathbb F_{2^{18}}^\*$* (the physically relevant claim, period $\mid 87381$), we need the cube root to live in $\mathbb F_{2^{18}}$ too:

> **Theorem (general condition).** Let $f\in R_m$ be $\sigma$-symmetric. Suppose there is a **$\sigma$-symmetric** $g\in R_m$ with
> $$f(x,y)\equiv g(x,y)\,g(x^2,y^2)\pmod{x^m-1,y^m-1}.$$
> Then for every $(j,k)$, $g(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)$ lies in the same field as $f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)$, and
> $$f(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)=g(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)^3.$$
> In particular, at $m=37$ every nonzero value is a cube in $\mathbb F_{2^{18}}^\*$, so its order divides $(2^{18}-1)/3=87381$.

*Proof.* $\sigma$-symmetry of $g$ gives $g(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)^{2^{18}}=g(\zeta^{-j},\zeta^{-k})=g(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)$, so $g(\zeta^j,\zeta^k)\in\mathbb F_{2^{18}}$. Then $f=g\cdot g^{(2)}$ evaluates to $g(P)\cdot g(P)^2=g(P)^3$. $\square$

**The "q‑th power" version:** replace $3=1+2$ by $q=1+2+\cdots+2^{r-1}=2^r-1$. If $f\equiv \prod_{i=0}^{r-1} g(x^{2^i},y^{2^i})$ then every value is a $(2^r-1)$‑th power. Cubes are the case $r=2$ (and $2^2-1=3$, matching the norm $\mathbb F_{2^{18}}\!\to\!\mathbb F_4$: $f(P)$ is a cube iff $N_{\mathbb F_{2^{18}}/\mathbb F_4}(f(P))=f(P)^{87381}=1$).

### Why 37 and not 9–161 generically

The property is "$f$ is a cube in $R_m$." As $m$ varies, $R_m$ breaks into fields of various orders; a generic $f$ hits the cube subgroup (index 3 when $3\mid 2^{\deg}-1$) in each factor independently, probability $\approx 3^{-(\#\text{factors})}$ — your "1 in 20,000." The five $f_i$ (and $t$) are engineered so the **global** element $f$ is a genuine cube $g\cdot g^{(2)}$ at $m=37$; at other moduli the orbit/field structure changes and no such symmetric $g$ exists, so the coincidence disappears. This is exactly what Lemma 1 predicts.

## What I proved vs. what remains

- **Proved (unconditional):** Lemmas 1–2 and the Theorem. These are the "reason." The precise general condition is the boxed identity with $g$ $\sigma$-symmetric.
- **Reduced to a finite check:** "every nonzero value of $f_i$ is a cube at $m=37$" $\iff$ "$\exists$ symmetric $g_i$ with $f_i\equiv g_i\,g_i^{(2)}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ x^{37}-1,y^{37}-1)$." Given your computed observation, such $g_i$ **must exist** (Lemma 1 guarantees a cube root in $R_{37}$; symmetrizing/averaging over $\sigma$ and the Frobenius-consistent choice yields a symmetric polynomial representative, since the values already lie in $\mathbb F_{2^{18}}$).
- **Not yet pinned down by me (I could not run code here):** the *explicit* coefficient list of each $g_i$. Constructing $g_i$ is a linear-algebra/cube-root interpolation over $R_{37}$; it is routine for your machine. I therefore register the predictions below rather than assert unverified identities.

## Predictions to register (testable)

1. For each of $t,f_1,\dots,f_5$ there exists a $\sigma$-symmetric Laurent polynomial $g$ over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$ with $f\equiv g(x,y)\,g(x^2,y^2)\pmod{x^{37}-1,y^{37}-1}$. (Find $g$ by solving the linear system "$f=g\cdot g^{(2)}$" over the $\mathbb F_{2^{18}}$ factors and interpolating; symmetrize.)
2. At the moduli $m\in\{9,11,\dots,161\}$ where the cube property fails, **no** symmetric $g$ with $f\equiv g\,g^{(2)}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ x^m-1,y^m-1)$ exists. (Direct corollary of Lemma 1 — a sharp, falsifiable prediction.)
3. **Generalization:** whenever your scan finds a new rule whose values all lie in the index‑$(2^r-1)$ subgroup, you will be able to write $f\equiv\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}g(x^{2^i},y^{2^i})$ with symmetric $g$. The period then divides $(2^{\deg}-1)/(2^r-1)$.
4. **Where the project may be under‑describing things:** the phenomenon is *not* "eigenvalues land in a subfield" (they generate $\mathbb F_{2^{18}}$, since $t(\zeta)$ has degree 18). It is strictly the multiplicative "$f$ is a cube/$(2^r-1)$‑th power in the group algebra." Stating it as subfield‑membership would be wrong; stating it as the boxed factorization is the correct invariant.
